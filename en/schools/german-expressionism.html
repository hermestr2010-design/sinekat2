German Expressionism

Distorted sets, stark shadows and tormented psychology turn the screen into a nightmare of the mind.

After the First World War, German studios made films in which the world is bent by the mind of its characters. Sets are painted with slanting walls and impossible shadows, actors move in stylised, almost trance-like ways, and the stories turn on madness, fear and authority. The movement began with The Cabinet of Dr. Caligari in 1920 and reached an international audience through Murnau's Nosferatu and Lang's Metropolis. When Hollywood hired many of its directors, cameramen and designers in the late 1920s, its look moved into horror films, thrillers and later film noir.

Where
Germany, mostly the UFA studios in Berlin
When
1920 to about 1930
Key figures
Robert Wiene, F.W. Murnau, Fritz Lang, Paul Wegener, Paul Leni, G.W. Pabst
Landmark films
The Cabinet of Dr. Caligari (1920), Nosferatu (1922), Metropolis (1927), The Golem (1920)
Legacy
Shaped horror, film noir and the visual style of directors from Hitchcock to Tim Burton

What to look for

  • Distorted, painted sets and sharp light-and-shadow contrast
  • Exaggerated, stylised acting
  • Subjects of madness, doubles, tyrants and the uncanny
