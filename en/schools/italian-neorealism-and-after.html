1940s – 1960s

Italian Neorealism and After

Real streets and non-professional actors in the ruins of war, followed by a more personal, modernist Italian art cinema.

In the ruins of 1945 Italy, Roberto Rossellini, Vittorio De Sica and Luchino Visconti took cameras into the streets and filmed ordinary people in real places, often with non-professional actors. Rome, Open City, Bicycle Thieves and La terra trema looked at poverty, war and unemployment without gloss or happy endings, and their method opened a path for filmmakers everywhere who lacked big budgets. By the late 1950s Italian cinema had moved to more personal and modern work: Fellini's dreams and memories, Antonioni's studies of emptiness and alienation, Pasolini's poetry and politics, and Visconti's historical epics.

Where
Italy, above all Rome
When
1943 to the 1960s
Key figures
Roberto Rossellini, Vittorio De Sica, Luchino Visconti, Federico Fellini, Michelangelo Antonioni, Pier Paolo Pasolini
Landmark films
Rome, Open City (1945), Bicycle Thieves (1948), La Dolce Vita (1960), L'Avventura (1960)
Legacy
Showed that cinema could be made on location with simple means, and inspired the French New Wave, Indian and Latin American cinema

What to look for

  • Real locations and non-professional actors
  • Stories of poverty, war and daily life, often without a neat ending
  • A simple, direct style that avoids glamour
