1920s

Soviet Montage

Meaning is built by colliding shots. Editing becomes a political and artistic weapon.

After the 1917 revolution, Soviet filmmakers asked what film could do that no other art could, and answered with editing. Lev Kuleshov showed that meaning comes from joining shots, not from the shots alone. Eisenstein built films from clashing images to move a crowd, Pudovkin used editing to follow one person's feelings, and Dovzhenko and Vertov brought poetry and the 'film-eye' to documentary. The state backed the cinema as a tool for the new society, which gave directors freedom to experiment until Stalin's demands for socialist realism ended the movement in the early 1930s.

Where
Soviet Union, mainly Moscow, Leningrad and Kyiv
When
1924 to the early 1930s
Key figures
Sergei Eisenstein, Vsevolod Pudovkin, Dziga Vertov, Alexander Dovzhenko, Lev Kuleshov
Landmark films
Battleship Potemkin (1925), Mother (1926), Man with a Movie Camera (1929), Earth (1930)
Legacy
Made editing a theory of film, and influenced propaganda, documentary, music video and action cinema

What to look for

  • Fast, rhythmic cutting that clashes one image against another
  • Crowds and groups as heroes instead of single stars
  • Bold camera angles and an openly political purpose
