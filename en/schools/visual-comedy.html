1910s – 1970s

Visual Comedy

Comedy built on physical performance, timing and inventive visual gags rather than dialogue.

Before sound, comedians told jokes with their bodies and the camera. Chaplin's Tramp mixed slapstick with tenderness, Keaton built impossibly exact stunts around his stone face, and Harold Lloyd's thrill comedies hung him from skyscraper clocks. Mack Sennett's Keystone studio taught Hollywood to cut gags for speed, and the tradition continued in Laurel and Hardy, in Jacques Tati's sound-era films that still use almost no dialogue, and in many later comedians. The art lies in timing, choreography and an eye for how objects and people can go wrong.

Where

Landmark films
The Kid (1921), The General (1926), Safety Last! (1923), Mr. Hulot's Holiday (1953)
Legacy
Set the template for physical comedy, from Chaplin's pathos to Tati's visual satire

What to look for

  • Gags built from timing, props and movement, not dialogue
  • A small, vulnerable hero against a big, hostile world
  • Stunts shot for real, in long takes that show the whole action
