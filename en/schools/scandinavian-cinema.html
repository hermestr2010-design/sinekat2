Scandinavian Cinema

From the Swedish silent golden age to Bergman, a tradition of stark landscapes, moral drama and psychological depth.

Scandinavia gave cinema two golden ages. In the 1910s and 1920s, Victor Sjöström and Mauritz Stiller made films with striking landscapes and psychological depth, and Carl Theodor Dreyer in Denmark made faces and silence into drama. After the war, Ingmar Bergman turned Swedish cinema into a stage for questions about faith, death and love, working with a close circle of actors and the photographer Sven Nykvist. A later generation led by Dogme 95 and by directors such as Lars von Trier and Thomas Vinterberg broke the rules again, and today Nordic cinema still ranges from austere art film to dark crime drama.

Where
Sweden, Denmark, Norway and Finland
When
1910s to the 1980s, with a later revival from the 1990s
Key figures
Victor Sjöström, Mauritz Stiller, Carl Theodor Dreyer, Ingmar Bergman, Bo Widerberg, Jan Troell
Landmark films
The Phantom Carriage (1921), The Passion of Joan of Arc (1928), The Seventh Seal (1957), Persona (1966)
Legacy
Set the standard for the personal, philosophical art film

What to look for

  • Spare, concentrated images and long close-ups of faces
  • Questions of faith, guilt, death and relationships
  • Strong use of nature and northern light
