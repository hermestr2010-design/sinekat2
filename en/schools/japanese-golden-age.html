1950s

Japanese Golden Age

Post-war Japan produces humanist dramas, samurai epics and quiet domestic masterpieces that reach the world.

In the 1950s, Japanese cinema, restarted after the war, became one of the world's richest. Kurosawa's Rashomon won the Golden Lion in Venice in 1951 and opened Western eyes to Japanese film, while Mizoguchi, in long, fluid takes, told sad stories about women under pressure, and Ozu, with a low, still camera, found the whole of life in family kitchens and train journeys. Naruse, Kobayashi, Ichikawa and Inagaki added their own voices. Studios such as Toho, Shochiku and Daiei produced films in large numbers, and the period combined period dramas, modern family stories and war films.

Where
Japan, mostly Tokyo and Kyoto studios
When
The 1950s, from the late 1940s to the early 1960s
Key figures
Akira Kurosawa, Kenji Mizoguchi, Yasujiro Ozu, Mikio Naruse, Masaki Kobayashi, Kon Ichikawa
Landmark films
Rashomon (1950), Tokyo Story (1953), Ugetsu (1953), Seven Samurai (1954)
Legacy
Introduced Japanese cinema to the world and influenced directors from Spielberg to Wim Wenders

What to look for

  • Long takes, careful composition and stillness
  • Family, duty and the changes of postwar Japan
  • Samurai and period films alongside quiet modern dramas
