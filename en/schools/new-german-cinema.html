1960s – 1980s

New German Cinema

Post-war German filmmakers confront history and identity in radical, personal ways.

In 1962 young German filmmakers signed the Oberhausen Manifesto, declaring that 'the old cinema is dead' and asking for a new kind that dealt honestly with their country's past. State funding and television co-productions made the work possible. Fassbinder, Herzog, Wenders, Schlöndorff, Kluge, von Trotta and Syberberg all made very different films, from Herzog's obsessive explorers to Fassbinder's cold melodramas and Wenders' road movies, but together they faced the legacy of Nazism and the postwar economic miracle. The movement ended around 1982 with Fassbinder's death, yet it restored Germany's place in world cinema.

What to look for

  • Confronting the Nazi past and postwar society
  • Strong personal visions and a mix of styles
  • Low budgets and support from state and television funding
